## Design direction

The result should resemble a clean preliminary ship-design drawing rather than a generic polygon with objects placed on it.

Aim for:

- a convincing continuous ship silhouette;
- a visibly designed bow and stern;
- an understandable relationship between hull, deck, superstructure, and funnels;
- restrained drafting detail;
- strong visual hierarchy;
- consistent line weights and dash patterns;
- clear separation between structural outlines, reference lines, and annotations;
- useful differences between hull forms;
- legibility at the panel's normal rendered size.

Do not pursue photorealism.

Do not add textures, gradients, drop shadows, glow, ornamental waves, fake rivets, or excessive decorative detail.

Do not draw details that imply unsupported gameplay data.

## 1. Refine the longitudinal hull silhouette

The profile must become a coherent closed hull shape, not a loose collection of independent deck, keel, stem, and stern lines.

Create or refine a pure geometry model that produces the longitudinal profile from normalized coordinates.

The silhouette should include:

- the upper deck or sheer line;
- the bow stem profile;
- the forefoot transition;
- the keel or bottom run;
- the stern cut-up;
- the stern profile or transom;
- closure back to the deck line.

Ensure positional and tangent continuity where practical. Avoid accidental corners unless the selected hull form intentionally requires one, such as a defined transom or ram knuckle.

Use smooth SVG paths with cubic or quadratic Bézier segments where appropriate.

Avoid excessive control points. Each segment should have a meaningful geometric role.

The hull should remain inside the view box with enough padding for strokes, labels, and markers.

## 2. Improve vertical proportions

Continue to use the established length scale for horizontal positioning.

Use the available draught, depth, and sheer values consistently when establishing vertical dimensions.

Do not claim that the drawing is an exact lines plan.

Introduce clear geometry boundaries between:

- baseline or keel datum;
- waterline;
- design draught;
- hull depth;
- deck or sheer line.

If physical dimensions are converted into screen coordinates, isolate the conversion in named scale helpers.

Prevent extreme or malformed data from producing:

- negative hull depth;
- inverted deck and keel;
- self-intersecting paths;
- stems outside the view box;
- funnels below the deck;
- superstructures floating above the deck;
- unreadably thin or tall hulls.

Use centralized, documented display clamps only where necessary for visual safety. Preserve raw domain values for labels and tooltips.

## 3. Give the stern deliberate geometry

The stern currently needs as much visual attention as the bow.

Create a deliberate stern treatment that integrates:

- the after sheer;
- the sternpost or transom;
- the keel cut-up;
- the termination of the waterline;
- the local deck overhang, where appropriate.

Do not use the same stern shape for every hull if existing hull-form information supports meaningful variation.

If no explicit stern-style field exists, derive only conservative differences from existing hull-form categories and document that they are schematic drawing choices.

Do not invent a new persistent domain field solely for this visual treatment unless repository architecture clearly requires it.

## 4. Refine hull-form-specific bows

Preserve the established hull-form character:

- torpedo boat: rounded spoon bow;
- coast-defence ship: blunt or plumb bow;
- cruiser: ram bow;
- pre-dreadnought: ram bow.

Make these profiles visually distinct in both plan and side view.

### Torpedo-boat spoon bow

The profile should have:

- a fine entry;
- a forward-rising curved stem;
- a smooth stem-to-forefoot transition;
- no accidental ram point.

### Coast-defence plumb bow

The profile should have:

- a visibly fuller forward body;
- a mostly upright stem;
- a controlled, rounded forefoot;
- no excessive knife-like point.

### Cruiser and pre-dreadnought ram bows

The profile should have:

- a readable projecting ram near or slightly below the waterline;
- an upper stem that returns toward the forecastle;
- a smooth connection between the ram, forefoot, and keel;
- restrained proportions that do not resemble a decorative spike.

The plan view and profile view must describe the same conceptual bow family. They do not need to be an exact three-dimensional projection, but they must not contradict each other.

Retain the existing area or coefficient behavior in the plan view. If geometry changes affect calculated area, update the calculation deliberately and extend its tests.

## 5. Improve deck and sheer presentation

Make the sheer visible without falsifying the underlying value.

Use the hull form's sheer data to drive the rise. The bow rise should remain stronger than the stern rise unless existing data specifies otherwise.

The sheer line should:

- be smooth;
- have no flat-to-curve kink;
- meet the bow and stern cleanly;
- support the placement of superstructure and funnels.

If the current sample cruiser's approximately 10 percent sheer remains subtle, improve visibility through drafting hierarchy and coordinate mapping rather than arbitrarily exaggerating the domain value.

A limited visual amplification may be used only if:

- it is isolated in a named display function;
- it is monotonic;
- it preserves zero as zero;
- it is documented as a display treatment;
- tests cover representative low, medium, and high values;
- the panel continues to disclose that the view is schematic.

## 6. Make the superstructure follow the deck

Do not render the superstructure as a generic rectangle that appears pasted over the hull.

Use the existing superstructure placement or zone as the longitudinal authority.

The profile superstructure should:

- rest directly on the local deck line;
- respect the deck's sheer;
- remain within the usable deck length;
- have a coherent forward and after termination;
- avoid colliding visually with the stem or stern;
- use restrained rounded corners only if consistent with the application's visual language.

It may use a small number of stepped masses to produce a more credible silhouette, but these steps must be deterministic drawing conventions. They must not imply component data that does not exist.

Prefer a helper such as a deck-height sampler so all deck-mounted features use the same underlying curve.

Do not duplicate the sheer formula in multiple components.

## 7. Refine funnel profiles and correspondence between views

Funnels are found through the component's `funnel` tag. Continue to use the real placed components.

In profile:

- place each funnel at its actual longitudinal position;
- anchor it to the local superstructure or deck elevation;
- use a believable tapered or slightly raked silhouette;
- prevent clipping;
- preserve separation where multiple funnels are close together;
- keep dimensions proportional to the local hull and superstructure rather than fixed arbitrary pixels.

In plan:

- retain a clear footprint for each placed funnel;
- ensure its longitudinal position corresponds to the profile;
- keep the footprint centered unless placement data explicitly provides transverse offset;
- distinguish funnel outlines from the centerline and superstructure outline.

Do not add funnels that are not present in the placement data.

Add a stable title, description, or accessible label where appropriate, without creating noisy repeated screen-reader output.

## 8. Add restrained naval-drafting detail

Add only details that materially improve interpretation.

Recommended details include:

- a clearly differentiated design waterline;
- short perpendicular or datum ticks at meaningful endpoints;
- a subtle baseline;
- a limited number of station ticks aligned between plan and profile;
- optional short vertical construction guides at major placement boundaries;
- a clear centre-of-buoyancy marker where relevant to the view;
- restrained dimension indicators for draught and depth if they remain legible.

Use no more detail than can be read at the rendered panel size.

Reference geometry should be visually secondary to the hull outline.

Use a consistent hierarchy similar to:

1. primary hull silhouette: strongest stroke;
2. deck edge and major fitted structures: medium stroke;
3. waterline and key datums: medium-light, differentiated by pattern or color;
4. station ticks and construction guides: lightest stroke;
5. labels and symbols: readable but subordinate.

Centralize stroke widths, dash arrays, marker sizes, and opacity values in named constants or tokens.

Use `vector-effect="non-scaling-stroke"` where appropriate so stroke weight remains stable under SVG scaling, provided it is compatible with the existing rendering and snapshot setup.

## 9. Improve composition of the two views

Treat the plan and profile as a coordinated drawing set.

They should:

- align major stations vertically;
- use consistent bow-left or bow-right orientation;
- have intentional spacing between views;
- have comparable internal padding;

Add concise view labels only if consistent with the existing UI. For example:

Do not embed large labels inside the SVG if ordinary accessible HTML captions provide better responsiveness and semantics.

The two views should not look like separate unrelated widgets.
