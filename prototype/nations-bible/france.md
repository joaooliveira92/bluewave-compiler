# **Phase 1 — Canonical Nation Identity**

# **French Republic, January 1880**

## **Relationship to Existing Canon: Britain and Germany**

France must occupy a third strategic identity, clearly distinct from both Britain and Germany.

- **Britain** is the mature maritime hegemon: global, route-based, commercially dependent, and overextended.
- **Germany** is the rising continental-industrial challenger: army-dominated, ambitious, concentrated, and undecided about naval destiny.
- **France** is the wounded republican great power: defeated on land in 1870–71, politically unstable but resilient, colonial-expansionist, technologically adventurous, and searching for naval methods to offset British superiority while containing German danger.

France is not a naval hegemon like Britain and not a newly unified industrial challenger like Germany. It is an old great power trying to rebuild prestige, security, and influence under the unstable institutions of the Third Republic.

France’s naval identity should be:

> **The experimental revanchist republic: a major colonial and naval power whose strategic imagination is divided between revenge against Germany, rivalry with Britain, Mediterranean security, colonial expansion, and asymmetric naval innovation.**

---

# **1\. Canonical Summary**

The **French Republic** in January 1880 is a major European power recovering from the trauma of the Franco-Prussian War and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine. Its political system is republican, parliamentary, factional, and periodically unstable. Its army remains central because Germany is the primary land threat. Yet France also possesses a large navy, a growing colonial empire, major Mediterranean interests, and a long tradition of maritime rivalry with Britain.

France should not feel like Britain with fewer ships. It should feel like a power pulled in several directions at once:

- continental revanche against Germany;
- colonial expansion in Africa and Asia;
- Mediterranean rivalry with Italy and Britain;
- protection of Atlantic and Channel coasts;
- possible commerce warfare against Britain;
- technological experimentation through torpedoes, cruisers, and submarines;
- domestic political instability affecting naval policy.

The French Navy was often regarded as one of the world’s major navies in this period, but French naval thinking was deeply shaped by the difficulty of matching the Royal Navy in battleships and by the search for alternative strategies such as torpedo warfare and commerce raiding.

---

# **2\. Strategic Identity**

## **The Wounded Great Power**

France begins 1880 as a state still shaped by defeat. The loss of Alsace-Lorraine to Germany creates a permanent strategic wound. Unlike Britain, France cannot think only in maritime terms. Unlike Germany, France is not trying to prove that it belongs among great powers; it is trying to recover a status it believes was unjustly diminished.

France’s strategic psychology is therefore built around:

- fear and resentment of Germany;
- desire to restore national prestige;
- anxiety over continental isolation;
- need to maintain a major army;
- desire for colonial compensation;
- continued rivalry with Britain;
- technological search for strategic leverage.

France is both defensive and revisionist. It wants security, but also restoration.

## **Dual Front: Germany and the Sea**

France’s key strategic dilemma is:

> **France needs the army to face Germany, but needs the navy to remain a world power.**

This makes France different from Britain and Germany.

- Britain’s navy is existential.
- Germany’s navy is aspirational.
- France’s navy is compensatory and prestige-restorative.

France cannot neglect the army because Germany is an immediate threat. But if France neglects the navy, it risks becoming merely a continental secondary power, losing colonial reach and maritime leverage.

## **Strategic Multiplicity**

France’s strategic world is fragmented:

- the Channel and Atlantic face Britain;
- the eastern frontier faces Germany;
- the Mediterranean links France to North Africa, Italy, and the Levant;
- colonies require cruisers and bases;
- commerce-raiding theory targets Britain;
- colonial expansion creates friction with Britain, Italy, and local powers.

France should therefore feel intellectually restless. It has more strategic theories than capacity to execute all of them simultaneously.

---

# **3\. Geographic Identity**

## **Metropolitan France: Exposed but Maritime**

France has both continental and maritime geography. It has vulnerable land borders, major Atlantic and Channel coasts, and a powerful Mediterranean frontage.

This differs sharply from Britain’s island security and Germany’s constrained two-sea access.

France has:

- Atlantic approaches;
- Channel ports;
- Mediterranean bases;
- proximity to North Africa;
- access to colonial routes;
- exposure to British sea power;
- direct land exposure to Germany.

This creates a navy that must think about both oceanic and enclosed-sea problems.

## **The Mediterranean as Strategic Core**

The Mediterranean is central to French naval identity. Toulon should be treated as a major naval center and Mediterranean strategy should matter from the start. French interests in Algeria, Tunisia, and the wider Mediterranean make the sea not merely a route but a theater of national influence.

French naval policy after the opening of the Suez Canal increasingly emphasized the Mediterranean and Toulon’s importance as a major base.

This gives France a different naval geography from Britain:

- Britain sees the Mediterranean as an imperial route to India.
- France sees it as a near-abroad, colonial frontier, and naval theater.

## **Atlantic and Channel Vulnerability**

France must also defend its Atlantic and Channel coasts. These waters are historically tied to rivalry with Britain and invasion fears. If Britain is hostile, French coasts and commerce are exposed. If Germany becomes the primary enemy, northern naval assets must still guard against raids, blockade, or opportunism.

This makes French naval deployment inherently divided.

---

# **4\. Imperial Identity**

## **Colonial Expansion as Compensation and Ambition**

France’s colonial empire is expanding rapidly in the late nineteenth century. Colonial expansion serves several purposes:

- prestige after defeat by Germany;
- commercial opportunity;
- naval basing;
- domestic political achievement;
- military manpower potential;
- rivalry with Britain and Italy;
- assertion that France remains a world power.

French colonialism should therefore be represented as both strategic and psychological. It is not as mature and route-integrated as Britain’s empire, but it is more substantial and serious than Germany’s prestige colonies.

France’s colonial policy in the early Third Republic, especially under Jules Ferry, was strongly associated with overseas expansion, even though it was politically controversial.

## **Empire of Bases and Prestige**

France should value colonies partly as **points d’appui** — support points from which cruisers can operate and French influence can be projected. French naval thought connected colonial bases with commerce warfare and blue-water operations against stronger maritime enemies.

This creates a middle identity:

- Britain’s empire is a global operating system.
- Germany’s empire is fragile prestige.
- France’s empire is an expanding strategic counterweight and prestige project.

France’s colonies should be useful but not as tightly integrated or logistically dominant as Britain’s.

---

# **5\. Political Identity**

## **The Parliamentary Republic Under Pressure**

France is governed by the Third Republic, a parliamentary republican system born after the collapse of the Second Empire during the Franco-Prussian War.

By January 1880, the republic has survived major threats but remains politically sensitive. The recent Boulangist crisis exposed the fragility of republican institutions and the continuing appeal of nationalism, authoritarianism, revanche, and anti-parliamentary politics. Boulanger’s movement peaked in 1889 and was associated with revenge against Germany and constitutional revision.

French politics should therefore generate:

- frequent cabinet changes;
- unstable naval priorities;
- factional budget fights;
- nationalist pressure after crises;
- colonial scandals;
- anti-German military pressure;
- republican suspicion of monarchist or Bonapartist officers;
- public enthusiasm for technological or patriotic naval programs.

## **Navy and Political Faction**

The French Navy should be politically more contested than Britain’s Royal Navy. In Britain, navalism broadly reinforces the state. In France, naval policy becomes one arena in a larger struggle over republic, empire, army, revenge, modernization, and social order.

France should have distinct political currents affecting naval policy:

- republican colonialists;
- army-first revanchists;
- naval traditionalists;
- Jeune École reformers;
- budget-cutting parliamentarians;
- nationalist press;
- colonial lobbyists;
- anti-British strategists;
- anti-German security hawks.

This is a crucial distinction: French naval policy should feel brilliant but unstable.

---

# **6\. Economic and Industrial Identity**

## **Strong but Uneven Industrial Power**

France is a major industrial and financial power, but its industrial trajectory is not identical to Britain or Germany.

- Britain is mature, global, and commercially maritime.
- Germany is rapidly accelerating, heavy-industrial, and technically systematized.
- France is sophisticated, innovative, financially capable, but politically and administratively uneven.

France has advanced engineering, naval architecture, artillery traditions, and state arsenals. But its naval production should sometimes suffer from fragmented policy, inconsistent programs, and design experimentation.

## **Innovation Without Consistency**

France should be very capable of producing advanced naval ideas. Historically, French naval thought and design were important in areas such as torpedo craft, protected cruisers, armored cruisers, and submarines. The Jeune École was especially associated with small heavily armed vessels, commerce raiding, torpedoes, and later submarine development as ways to offset British battleship superiority.

However, France should not always convert innovation into coherent fleet structure. Its weakness is not lack of creativity; it is strategic inconsistency.

---

# **7\. Naval Institutional Identity**

## **A Great Navy in Search of Doctrine**

The French Navy is large, prestigious, technically inventive, and historically important. But by 1880 it is divided between competing visions:

1. traditional battle fleet;
2. coastal defense;
3. torpedo flotillas;
4. commerce raiding;
5. colonial cruisers;
6. Mediterranean power projection;
7. experimental undersea warfare.

This makes France one of the most interesting playable nations.

Britain’s navy has a dominant institutional logic: command of the sea.  
Germany’s navy has an emerging logic: concentrated rise.  
France’s navy has a contested logic: how to compensate for strategic inferiority without surrendering great-power status.

## **The Jeune École**

The **Jeune École** should be central to French naval identity in 1880, but it must not fully replace all other doctrines.

Its core ideas:

- small, heavily armed vessels can threaten large battleships;
- torpedo boats can offset battleship inferiority;
- commerce raiding can attack Britain’s economic lifelines;
- fast cruisers can conduct guerre de course;
- technological disruption can compensate for numerical inferiority.

The Jeune École developed among French naval theorists in the nineteenth century and became influential in the French Navy by the late nineteenth century, especially under Admiral Théophile Aube.

Design implication: France should have strong access to asymmetric naval paths, but those paths should create doctrinal and procurement tension rather than being a simple advantage.

## **Officer Corps and Institutional Friction**

French naval officers should be divided by mission and doctrine:

- traditional fleet officers favor battleships and line-of-battle prestige;
- colonial officers favor cruisers and distant stations;
- Jeune École advocates favor torpedoes, speed, commerce warfare, and technological disruption;
- administrators worry about budgets and shipyard efficiency.

This internal conflict should affect future doctrine, technology, and procurement phases.

---

# **8\. Naval Strategic Doctrine**

## **Anti-British Maritime Logic**

Even though Germany is France’s primary land threat, Britain remains the benchmark enemy at sea. French naval thought in this period often assumed that a future naval war could be against Britain, and the Jeune École was explicitly shaped by the problem of challenging a larger Royal Navy.

France cannot realistically outbuild Britain battleship-for-battleship. Therefore, plausible French doctrine emphasizes:

- torpedo defense of coasts;
- commerce raiding;
- fast cruisers;
- colonial bases;
- attacks on trade routes;
- Mediterranean pressure;
- technological disruption.

## **Anti-German Continental Constraint**

Against Germany, the navy has a different role. Germany is primarily a continental threat in 1880\. The French army carries the burden of revanche. But the navy may still matter by:

- defending coasts;
- threatening German trade;
- supporting alliances;
- pressuring German overseas possessions;
- containing German naval growth;
- protecting French colonial communications.

France should therefore be able to pursue either anti-British maritime strategy or anti-German security strategy, but not always both efficiently.

## **Guerre de Course**

Commerce warfare should be a canonical French strategic option. It should be especially relevant against Britain, whose merchant marine and trade routes are central vulnerabilities.

Fast cruisers such as the French armored cruiser _Dupuy de Lôme_, laid down in 1888, reflected commerce-raiding ideas associated with the Jeune École.

However, commerce warfare must not become fantasy. It requires:

- bases;
- coal;
- intelligence;
- cruiser endurance;
- diplomatic tolerance;
- repair access;
- raider survivability.

France can threaten British trade, but cannot automatically destroy it.

---

# **9\. Ship Design Identity**

## **General Design Philosophy**

French ship design should feel innovative, varied, and sometimes strategically inconsistent.

French designers should tend toward:

- experimentation;
- speed;
- unusual layouts;
- strong individual concepts;
- torpedo integration;
- cruiser warfare;
- protected and armored cruisers;
- coastal defense solutions;
- interest in submarines later;
- less standardization than Britain or Germany.

This is not “bad design.” It is design driven by doctrinal debate and technological ambition.

## **Battleships**

French battleships should be important but politically and doctrinally contested. Traditional naval officers want them because they symbolize great-power status and remain central to fleet battle. Jeune École advocates question whether France can afford battleship competition with Britain.

French battleship design may therefore show:

- powerful but sometimes idiosyncratic layouts;
- high individual quality;
- slower class standardization;
- debate over armor versus speed versus armament;
- tension between prestige and utility.

France should not be prohibited from building strong battle fleets. But doing so should compete with cruisers, torpedo craft, colonial needs, and army priorities.

## **Cruisers**

Cruisers are crucial for France.

French cruisers should serve:

- colonial presence;
- commerce raiding;
- reconnaissance;
- Mediterranean operations;
- fast independent missions;
- prestige visits;
- colonial crisis response.

Unlike British cruisers, which maintain an existing trade empire, French cruisers should be more offensive and opportunistic. They are tools of expansion, pressure, and strategic compensation.

## **Torpedo Craft**

France should be one of the most important early adopters and doctrinal users of torpedo craft. The Jeune École strongly emphasized small torpedo-armed vessels as a means to threaten larger ships.

French torpedo craft should be politically attractive because they promise:

- cheap defense;
- technological modernity;
- anti-battleship capability;
- coastal security;
- democratic/republican “many small ships” symbolism;
- a way to challenge Britain without matching British battleship spending.

But they should also create risks:

- limited seaworthiness;
- command-and-control difficulty;
- overconfidence in unproven tactics;
- neglect of fleet coherence;
- vulnerability to destroyer countermeasures.

## **Submarines**

France should have a natural path toward early submarine interest. The Jeune École and French interest in technological alternatives make submarines historically plausible as a strong future French development area. France was active in submarine development and by the early twentieth century had an effective submarine arm.

In 1880, submarines should not dominate, but France should be culturally and doctrinally receptive to them.

---

# **10\. Industrial and Shipbuilding Identity**

## **State Arsenals and Private Industry**

France should have a mixed naval-industrial system with important state arsenals and private capability. Its shipyards are capable of advanced warship construction, but procurement may be affected by political shifts, doctrinal disputes, regional interests, and inconsistent programs.

Compared with Britain:

- less globally scaled;
- less standardized;
- less merchant-marine-driven;
- more politically volatile.

Compared with Germany:

- more naval tradition;
- more colonial need;
- less methodical industrial-state integration;
- more design experimentation.

## **Construction Pattern**

France’s construction pattern should tend toward:

- varied ship types;
- experimental prototypes;
- doctrinally driven bursts;
- cruiser and torpedo emphasis when Jeune École influence rises;
- battleship revival when traditionalists regain control;
- uneven class continuity.

This should make France replayable: different political and doctrinal outcomes can produce very different fleets.

---

# **11\. Technological Identity**

## **Technological Adventurism**

France should be one of the most technologically interesting nations in the game. Its naval identity should include a willingness to explore disruptive tools earlier or more enthusiastically than Britain.

Strong areas:

- torpedoes;
- torpedo boats;
- fast cruisers;
- protected cruisers;
- armored cruisers;
- submarine experimentation;
- naval artillery concepts;
- coastal defense;
- alternative fleet doctrines.

The Jeune École’s embrace of torpedoes, small craft, commerce raiders, and submarines makes France the natural home of disruptive naval paths.

## **Weakness: Integration**

France’s weakness should not be low technology. It should be integration.

French technology can be excellent, but may suffer from:

- too many competing prototypes;
- inconsistent doctrine;
- political disruption;
- lack of standardization;
- maintenance complexity;
- overinvestment in fashionable theories;
- underinvestment in mundane fleet logistics.

France should be brilliant, dangerous, and sometimes self-disruptive.

---

# **12\. Diplomatic Identity**

## **Germany as Existential Land Rival**

Germany is France’s central security problem. The memory of 1870–71 and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine create permanent hostility. French politics includes strong revanchist currents, and figures such as Boulanger gained popularity partly through promises of revenge against Germany.

France should seek ways to avoid isolation against Germany. This makes alliance diplomacy essential.

## **Britain as Maritime Rival and Colonial Competitor**

Britain is not France’s land enemy, but it is the dominant naval and colonial rival. French naval theory often treated Britain as the problem to be solved at sea. Colonial expansion, Mediterranean policy, Egypt, Africa, and Asian interests can all generate friction.

Before the Entente Cordiale, Franco-British relations were not naturally friendly; colonial rivalry remained significant and would later culminate in crises such as Fashoda in 1898\.

France’s diplomacy should therefore be triangular:

- Germany is the security enemy.
- Britain is the maritime/colonial rival.
- Russia is the likely strategic partner.

## **Opportunistic Alignment**

France should be highly motivated to find allies, especially against Germany. Unlike Britain, France cannot rely on splendid isolation. Unlike Germany, France cannot easily prevent encirclement through central position. France must compensate through diplomacy, finance, colonial bargaining, and naval cooperation where possible.

---

# **13\. Colonial and Overseas Policy Identity**

## **Expansionist Republic**

France should be an active colonial player. Its colonies are not merely decorative. They provide:

- prestige;
- bases;
- manpower potential;
- economic claims;
- diplomatic friction;
- naval missions;
- competition with Britain and Italy;
- domestic political controversy.

French colonial expansion should be more assertive than Germany’s and less structurally mature than Britain’s.

## **Colonial Bases as Strategic Leverage**

French colonial bases should support:

- cruiser operations;
- Mediterranean control;
- commerce raiding possibilities;
- African and Asian influence;
- pressure against British routes;
- prestige projection.

But they should also require defense. France has enough empire to create responsibility, but not enough naval supremacy to make it secure.

---

# **14\. Intelligence and Threat Perception**

France should track two different categories of threat.

## **Continental Threats**

- German army strength;
- German mobilization;
- German naval development;
- alliances involving Germany;
- Italian alignment with Germany or Austria-Hungary;
- Alsace-Lorraine tensions.

## **Maritime Threats**

- British battleship numbers;
- British cruiser deployments;
- British Mediterranean posture;
- Italian naval growth;
- German North Sea naval expansion;
- colonial vulnerabilities;
- commerce-raiding opportunities;
- torpedo and destroyer countermeasures.

France should be prone to strategic anxiety because it has multiple plausible enemies but insufficient resources to dominate all theaters.

---

# **15\. Social and Cultural Identity**

## **Republic, Revenge, and Prestige**

France’s national identity in 1880 combines republican patriotism, humiliation from defeat, colonial pride, scientific modernity, and political division.

The navy can symbolize:

- national recovery;
- technological brilliance;
- colonial grandeur;
- revenge indirectly through renewed power;
- republican modernity;
- resistance to British maritime dominance.

But the army often occupies the emotional center of revanche. This makes the navy important but not uncontested.

## **Public Debate and Naval Theory**

French naval doctrine should be unusually public and ideological. The Jeune École was not merely a technical doctrine; it existed in a broader environment of press debate, republican politics, technological optimism, and criticism of traditional naval hierarchies.

This supports a gameplay identity in which France gets powerful doctrinal movements that can change procurement priorities.

---

# **16\. AI Personality Definition**

If controlled by AI, France should behave as a **restless revanchist innovator**.

## **Strategic Priorities**

French AI should prioritize:

1. preventing German dominance;
2. avoiding diplomatic isolation;
3. maintaining a credible major navy;
4. defending Mediterranean interests;
5. expanding or preserving colonies;
6. investing in cruisers and torpedo forces;
7. seeking technological offsets against Britain;
8. balancing army and navy demands;
9. exploiting colonial opportunities;
10. recovering national prestige.

## **AI Behavioral Traits**

French AI should be:

- anti-German by default;
- wary of Britain;
- colonial and prestige-sensitive;
- technologically experimental;
- politically volatile;
- willing to use asymmetric naval strategies;
- inclined toward alliance-seeking;
- prone to doctrinal swings.

## **AI Strategic Errors**

France should sometimes:

- overinvest in theories before they are proven;
- neglect battleships during torpedo enthusiasm;
- neglect torpedo defense during battleship revival;
- provoke Britain in colonial disputes;
- split resources between too many theaters;
- build non-standard ship classes;
- allow domestic crises to interrupt programs;
- underestimate logistics for commerce warfare.

These errors should arise from France’s identity, not arbitrary weakness.

---

# **17\. Player Fantasy**

Playing France should feel like leading a brilliant but divided republic trying to regain strategic initiative.

The French player fantasy is:

- avenge humiliation without reckless war;
- outthink stronger naval rivals;
- use technology to alter naval balance;
- build a colonial empire that supports strategy;
- contain Germany through alliances and deterrence;
- challenge British dominance without necessarily matching it;
- decide whether France’s navy is a battle fleet, raiding force, colonial navy, or technological disruptor.

The French campaign should ask:

> Can you turn political instability, colonial ambition, and technological experimentation into a coherent strategy before Germany or Britain forces the issue?

# **18\. Direct Comparison: France vs Britain vs Germany**

## **Strategic Identity**

**Britain:** Maritime hegemon defending an existing global order.  
**Germany:** Continental-industrial challenger deciding whether to become a naval power.  
**France:** Defeated old great power using navy, colonies, alliances, and technology to restore status.

## **Naval Purpose**

**Britain:** Navy is existential infrastructure.  
**Germany:** Navy is aspirational world-power instrument.  
**France:** Navy is strategic compensation, colonial tool, and technological equalizer.

## **Main Anxiety**

**Britain:** Losing command of sea routes.  
**Germany:** Encirclement and insufficient recognition.  
**France:** German revanche pressure plus British maritime superiority.

## **Empire**

**Britain:** Global operating system.  
**Germany:** Prestige-heavy, logistically fragile.  
**France:** Expanding strategic counterweight and prestige project.

## **Doctrine**

**Britain:** Command of the sea, blockade, trade protection.  
**Germany:** Concentrated fleet, home-water deterrence, future risk strategy.  
**France:** Jeune École, torpedo warfare, commerce raiding, colonial cruisers, contested battle fleet.

## **Technology**

**Britain:** Broad, pragmatic, incremental.  
**Germany:** Methodical, industrial, concentrated.  
**France:** Experimental, disruptive, unevenly integrated.

## **Gameplay Feel**

**Britain:** Manage overstretched supremacy.  
**Germany:** Build focused naval relevance.  
**France:** Forge coherence from competing strategic visions.

# **19\. Design Constraints for Future Phases**

## **Geography Phase**

France must be modeled as both continental and maritime: Atlantic, Channel, Mediterranean, and colonial routes all matter.

## **Territories Phase**

French colonies must support prestige, bases, Mediterranean strategy, and commerce-raiding potential, but should not equal Britain’s mature global system.

## **Resources Phase**

France should have sufficient industrial and colonial-resource ambitions, but resource access should be less globally secure than Britain’s and less heavy-industrial explosive than Germany’s.

## **Government Phase**

French government must include cabinet instability, parliamentary politics, nationalist pressure, republican factionalism, colonial lobbies, and army-navy budget tension.

## **Industry Phase**

France must have advanced but uneven industrial capacity, strong arsenals, technical creativity, and occasional procurement inconsistency.

## **Shipbuilding Phase**

French shipbuilding should support experimentation, varied classes, cruisers, torpedo craft, and battleships, but with lower standardization than Britain or Germany.

## **Naval Infrastructure Phase**

France must have strong metropolitan bases, especially Mediterranean infrastructure, plus colonial support points of uneven quality.

## **Technology Phase**

France must be strong in torpedoes, small craft, cruisers, submarine experimentation, and disruptive naval concepts.

## **Doctrine Phase**

France must include competing doctrines: traditional battle fleet, Jeune École, guerre de course, coastal defense, colonial cruiser strategy.

## **Diplomacy Phase**

France must be structurally anti-German, wary of Britain, and motivated to seek alliances to avoid isolation.

## **Scenario Phase**

France’s January 1880 setup must reflect the recent Boulangist crisis, unresolved revanche, colonial expansion, naval doctrinal conflict, and the search for alternatives to British-style naval supremacy.

# **20\. Historical Assumptions**

## **Assumption 1 — France Is a Major Naval Power but Not a Naval Hegemon**

France is treated as one of the principal navies of the era, but unable to match Britain’s global maritime system directly. This justifies asymmetric and commerce-raiding doctrines.

## **Assumption 2 — The Franco-Prussian War Still Defines France**

France’s defeat in 1870–71 and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine are assumed to shape politics, army priorities, diplomacy, and nationalist pressure in 1880\. The popularity of Boulangism demonstrates the political force of revanche.

## **Assumption 3 — Jeune École Is Influential but Not Total**

The Jeune École is treated as a major influence on French naval policy, especially in torpedo craft, commerce raiding, and technological alternatives, but not as the only doctrine in the navy.

## **Assumption 4 — France’s Colonies Are Strategically Important but Incomplete**

French colonies are assumed to provide prestige, bases, and strategic options, especially for cruisers and Mediterranean policy, but not the same mature global logistics network as Britain.

## **Assumption 5 — France’s Core Gameplay Is Strategic Coherence**

France’s challenge is not lack of ideas or lack of capability. It is turning many competing priorities into one coherent naval strategy.

---

# **21\. Canonical One-Sentence Identity**

> **The French Republic in January 1880 is a politically unstable but resilient great power, wounded by defeat against Germany, active in colonial expansion, wary of British maritime supremacy, and unusually open to disruptive naval doctrines that promise to restore French influence without simply copying the Royal Navy.**
