# **Phase 1 — Canonical Nation Identity**

# **Empire of Japan, January 1880**

# **1\. Canonical Summary**

The **Empire of Japan** in January 1880 is a rapidly modernizing island empire in the Meiji era. It has recently emerged from the Tokugawa order and is still transforming its institutions, industry, military, education, law, and state structure. Its leaders understand that modern naval power is essential if Japan is to avoid becoming a subordinate state in an imperial world.

The Imperial Japanese Navy was formed after the Meiji Restoration and developed through intense modernization, foreign study, imported ships, officer training, and institutional reform. During the 1870s Japan’s naval expansion was limited by financial strain and domestic unrest, but in the 1880s naval spending and expansion accelerated, especially after crises involving Korea and China.

Japan’s 1880 identity should therefore be:

> **A young maritime state with old strategic fears and new imperial ambitions, building a modern navy as insurance against foreign domination and as the instrument of regional ascent.**

Japan begins behind Britain, France, and probably Germany in industrial depth and naval scale. But it should possess unusually high strategic focus, institutional discipline, learning speed, and political willingness to sacrifice for modernization.

---

# **2\. Strategic Identity**

## **Island State Under Pressure**

Japan’s geography creates a naval imperative. It is an island country close to Korea, China, Russia’s Far Eastern interests, and Western imperial routes. Unlike Britain, Japan does not yet command a global base network. Unlike the United States, it cannot rely on continental depth. Unlike Germany and France, it cannot assume that land armies alone secure national survival.

Japan’s state security depends on controlling or at least denying hostile naval movement in nearby waters:

- the Home Islands;
- the Korea Strait;
- the Yellow Sea approaches;
- the Sea of Japan;
- the Ryukyus;
- Formosa/Taiwan as a future strategic objective;
- approaches toward China and Korea;
- northern waters facing Russia.

Japan’s core strategic question is:

> **Can Japan become strong enough at sea to prevent stronger empires from deciding East Asia’s future without it?**

## **Regional Sea Power Before Global Sea Power**

Japan’s early naval ambitions should be regional, not global. Its first aim is not to police oceans like Britain or project hemispheric dominance like the United States. Its first aim is to secure influence in East Asia.

Japan’s likely strategic priorities are:

1. protect the Home Islands;
2. prevent Korea from becoming a hostile foreign-controlled bridgehead;
3. challenge Qing influence in Korea;
4. deter or prepare against Russia’s Far Eastern expansion;
5. renegotiate Japan’s status among Western powers;
6. acquire or secure forward naval bases;
7. build domestic naval industry;
8. master modern fleet tactics and technology.

This creates a distinctive Japan:

- Britain defends a world empire.
- Germany seeks recognition as a world power.
- France seeks restored prestige and colonial leverage.
- The United States awakens to overseas ambition.
- Japan fights to become secure in its own region before others dominate it.

## **Strategic Dilemma**

Japan’s central strategic dilemma is:

> **Japan needs a modern navy before it has the industrial base of a modern naval great power.**

This is the opposite of the United States, which has immense industry before full naval doctrine. Japan has urgent doctrine and strategic need before it has mature industrial capacity.

Japan must import, learn, copy, adapt, and eventually produce. Its gameplay should emphasize disciplined prioritization: every ship, yard, training mission, and foreign advisor matters.

---

# **3\. Geographic Identity**

## **Maritime Insularity**

Japan is naturally maritime. Its islands give defensive depth against invasion, but also make sea control essential. A hostile fleet can isolate Japan, threaten trade, cut imported materials, pressure coastal cities, and block military movement to Korea or China.

Japan’s geography is therefore not global like Britain’s, but it is intensely naval.

Japanese naval geography should be built around:

- short but strategically decisive sea lanes;
- narrow straits;
- island chains;
- nearby continental coasts;
- forward basing problems;
- weather and seasonal conditions;
- need to move armies by sea while not controlling armies directly;
- vulnerability to blockade if industry depends on imports.

The player does not directly control armies, but Japan’s navy must enable any overseas military policy. If the navy cannot move, protect, or supply expeditionary forces, Japan’s continental ambitions fail.

## **Korea as Strategic Keystone**

Korea must be central to Japan’s identity even before later territorial phases define it in detail. Japan’s leaders historically saw Korea as strategically vital because it lies close to the Japanese Home Islands and can serve either as a buffer or as a threat if controlled by a hostile power.

The 1882 Imo Incident in Korea, in which Japanese personnel and the legation were attacked and Japan dispatched a naval squadron back to Seoul, helped convince Japanese leaders that delaying military and naval expansion was dangerous. China’s ability to intervene strongly in Korea reinforced Japanese anxiety about Qing influence.

Design implication: Japan should be uniquely sensitive to Korean instability, foreign influence in Korea, and naval access to the Korea Strait.

## **China and Russia as Geographic Pressures**

Japan’s early naval planning should be shaped by two regional giants:

- **Qing China**, older, larger, and still formally influential in Korea.
- **Russia**, continental, expanding, and potentially dangerous in the north and Far East.

Japan cannot defeat these powers by land mass. It must use maritime mobility, operational concentration, modernization, and timing.

---

# **4\. Imperial Identity**

## **Not Yet a Mature Colonial Empire**

In January 1880, Japan is not yet an overseas empire comparable to Britain or France. It has ambitions, strategic anxieties, and regional interests, but its formal imperial structure is still in formation.

This matters. Japan should not begin with a large colonial network. Its expansionist path should emerge through:

- Korea;
- Taiwan/Formosa possibilities;
- Ryukyu consolidation;
- Pacific island interest later;
- treaty-port and China policy;
- competition with Russia;
- desire for recognition as a “civilized” great power.

Japan’s empire begins as a security project as much as a prestige project.

## **Expansion as Security**

Where Germany’s colonies are prestige-heavy and Britain’s are a global operating system, Japan’s early expansion should be framed as **defensive imperialism**:

> Japan expands outward because remaining confined to the Home Islands may leave it vulnerable to stronger empires.

This does not make Japanese expansion benign. It means the internal logic is different. Japan’s leaders may frame regional dominance as necessary for survival and equality.

## **Recognition and Treaty Revision**

Japan’s foreign policy is also shaped by the desire to be recognized as a modern sovereign power. Naval strength, constitutional reform, industrial modernization, and military success all contribute to the diplomatic goal of revising unequal relationships with Western powers.

The Meiji Constitution was promulgated in 1889 and came into effect in 1880, forming part of Japan’s broader project of building modern state institutions recognizable to Western powers. [\[ndl.go.jp\]](https://www.ndl.go.jp/modern/e/cha2/index.html),

# **5\. Political Identity**

## **Meiji Constitutional Empire**

Japan in January 1880 is entering a new constitutional phase. The Meiji Constitution had been promulgated on February 11, 1889, and came into effect on November 29, 1880, creating the Imperial Diet and a constitutional monarchy structure in which the emperor retained extensive sovereign and military authority. [\[ndl.go.jp\]](https://www.ndl.go.jp/modern/e/cha2/index.html),

This should make Japan politically distinct.

Japan is not a parliamentary navalist state like Britain.  
It is not a federal imperial state like Germany.  
It is not a factional parliamentary republic like France.  
It is not a congressional federal republic like the United States.

Japan is a modernizing imperial state where oligarchs, military leaders, court authority, ministries, and emerging parliamentary politics coexist uneasily.

## **Genrō, Ministries, and Imperial Authority**

Japan’s political system should include powerful elder statesmen and Meiji oligarchs who guide modernization from above. Naval policy should depend on elite consensus, army-navy competition, fiscal capacity, and the perceived need to gain equality with Western powers.

The first Imperial Diet opened in 1880, creating a formal legislature with a House of Peers and House of Representatives, but the government maintained strong distance from political parties and remained heavily influenced by oligarchic leadership. [\[ndl.go.jp\]](https://www.ndl.go.jp/modern/e/cha2/index.html),

Design implication: Japan should have parliamentary pressure, but not Western-style parliamentary control over the navy.

## **Army-Navy Relationship**

Japan’s military modernization includes both army and navy. The army matters because Japan’s regional goals involve Korea and the Asian mainland. But the navy is indispensable because all overseas military action requires command of sea communications.

This creates a strong internal strategic tension:

- the army wants continental capability;
- the navy wants ships, bases, and sea control;
- the state needs both but cannot afford everything;
- industrial limits force prioritization.

Unlike Germany, where the army is already the unquestioned senior instrument of continental power, Japan’s army and navy both represent modernization and survival. Their rivalry should be important but not identical to Germany’s.

---

# **6\. Economic and Industrial Identity**

## **Modernizing but Resource-Constrained**

Japan is industrializing rapidly, but in January 1880 it does not yet possess the coal, steel, shipbuilding, heavy gun, machinery, and financial depth of Britain, Germany, France, or the United States.

This is central to Japan’s gameplay identity.

Japan should have:

- strong state-directed modernization;
- rapidly improving education and technical capacity;
- limited heavy industry;
- dependence on imports for advanced naval equipment;
- need for foreign-built ships;
- selective domestic capacity;
- intense budgetary pressure;
- willingness to sacrifice for strategic modernization.

During the early Meiji naval buildup, Japan had to purchase or construct vessels abroad and focus heavily on training and officer development before it could fully support major naval expansion domestically. ,

## **Learning State**

Japan’s economy and navy should be modeled as a learning system.

Its advantages are:

- speed of institutional learning;
- disciplined adoption of foreign technology;
- willingness to send students and officers abroad;
- ability to integrate foreign models into Japanese institutions;
- strong elite commitment to national modernization.

Its disadvantages are:

- limited domestic heavy industry;
- fiscal burden;
- reliance on imported ships and components;
- vulnerability to foreign suppliers;
- smaller industrial margin for mistakes.

The Japanese player should feel that every modernization decision has opportunity cost.

---

# **7\. Naval Institutional Identity**

## **Young Navy With Serious Purpose**

The Imperial Japanese Navy is young but unusually purposeful. It was created after the Meiji Restoration and developed through modernization, Western influence, foreign ship purchases, training, and institutional reform.

Japanese naval culture should feel:

- disciplined;
- ambitious;
- status-conscious;
- technically adaptive;
- eager to learn from stronger navies;
- regionally focused;
- institutionally young;
- strategically urgent.

The IJN should not yet have Britain’s operational experience, France’s doctrinal tradition, or Germany’s heavy-industrial backing. But it should possess high motivation and rapid professionalization.

## **Foreign Models and Selective Adaptation**

Japan should not copy a single foreign navy mechanically. It should absorb from multiple sources:

- British naval professionalism and seamanship;
- French Jeune École influence in the 1880s;
- European shipbuilding and artillery;
- Western naval education and staff methods;
- its own regional strategic requirements.

The French Jeune École influenced the Imperial Japanese Navy in the 1880s, especially because its emphasis on torpedo craft and smaller vessels appealed to navies trying to compensate for weaker battleship forces. ,

But Japan must adapt these ideas to local conditions. Its most important theater is not global commerce warfare against Britain; it is regional conflict in East Asian waters.

---

# **8\. Naval Strategic Doctrine**

## **Concentrated Regional Superiority**

Japan’s most plausible naval doctrine in 1880 is not global cruiser warfare or worldwide sea control. It is concentrated regional superiority.

Japan should seek to:

- concentrate modern ships quickly;
- defeat a regional fleet before larger powers intervene;
- secure sea lanes to Korea or China;
- protect troop movements without directly controlling armies;
- use mobility and training to offset numerical inferiority;
- exploit shorter lines near the Home Islands;
- deny enemy access to Japanese waters.

This creates a tactical-operational identity: Japan should be good at preparing for decisive regional campaigns.

## **Anti-Qing and Anti-Russian Foundations**

Japan’s navy should be built with Qing China and Russia in mind, though Britain, France, and the United States remain important diplomatic and technological reference points.

The 1882 Korean crisis demonstrated the regional competition between Japan and China and contributed to Japanese military expansion concerns.

Against Qing China, Japan needs speed, training, modern ships, and the ability to strike before China can coordinate its larger resources.

Against Russia, Japan must worry about northern approaches, future Russian bases, and the danger of a continental empire projecting naval power into East Asia.

## **Limited Fleet, High Readiness**

Because Japan cannot afford a large global fleet, it should value readiness, training, concentration, and modernization quality. A smaller Japanese fleet should be dangerous if well prepared, but brittle if losses mount.

Design implication: Japan should reward careful procurement and training rather than raw mass.

---

# **9\. Ship Design Identity**

## **General Design Philosophy**

Japanese ship design in 1880 should be defined by selective modernization under constraint.

Japan should favor:

- modern protected cruisers;
- torpedo craft;
- fast, efficient combatants;
- ships suitable for regional waters;
- imported advanced designs;
- high crew training;
- practical concentration of force;
- avoidance of unaffordable vanity fleets;
- later progression toward battleships as industry and prestige grow.

Japan should not begin able to build a first-rate battle fleet domestically. It should begin by choosing where foreign purchases, domestic construction, and doctrine can produce maximum strategic effect.

## **Battleships**

Battleships should be aspirational in 1880\. Japan understands their prestige and combat value, but cannot yet treat them as casually affordable. Early Japanese battleship policy should be shaped by:

- foreign purchase;
- budget sacrifice;
- diplomatic dependence;
- limited dockyard capacity;
- desire for great-power recognition;
- need to counter Chinese and Russian capital ships.

A Japanese battleship should feel like a national project, not routine procurement.

## **Cruisers**

Cruisers are central to early Japan.

Japanese cruisers should serve:

- regional scouting;
- fleet concentration;
- Korea and China operations;
- commerce protection in nearby waters;
- diplomatic flag-showing;
- limited overseas training;
- rapid response to crises.

Unlike British cruisers, Japanese cruisers do not police a global empire. Unlike French cruisers, they are not primarily commerce-raiding weapons against Britain. They are instruments of regional mobility and modernization.

## **Torpedo Craft**

Torpedo craft should matter greatly to Japan because they offer a way to threaten larger ships and defend nearby waters at lower cost.

French Jeune École ideas were attractive to younger navies trying to offset battleship inferiority through torpedoes and smaller craft. ,

For Japan, torpedo craft should be:

- coastal defense tools;
- night-attack weapons;
- fleet auxiliaries;
- equalizers against larger enemies;
- politically attractive because they are cheaper than battleships.

But their limits must be real:

- short range;
- weather sensitivity;
- training demands;
- communications difficulty;
- risk of overconfidence.

---

# **10\. Industrial and Shipbuilding Identity**

## **Dependency and Ambition**

Japan’s naval industry should begin dependent but ambitious. It can build some ships and maintain a growing fleet, but advanced warship construction still depends on foreign technology, imported components, and learning from overseas yards.

Japan’s shipbuilding identity should be:

- constrained in 1880;
- rapidly improving;
- state-supported;
- strategically prioritized;
- strongly linked to education and industry policy;
- dependent on foreign purchases for high-end capability;
- determined to achieve autonomy over time.

## **Naval Industry as Nation-Building**

For Japan, naval industry is not merely military procurement. It is part of national modernization.

Dockyards, arsenals, engineering schools, naval academies, and foreign contracts all serve the same larger goal: creating a state that cannot be coerced by Western or regional powers.

This differs from Britain’s mature public-private naval economy, Germany’s heavy-industrial naval mobilization, France’s experimental arsenals, and America’s scalable private industry.

Japan’s naval industry is developmental.

---

# **11\. Technological Identity**

## **Fast Learner, Selective Adopter**

Japan should be one of the fastest-learning naval powers in the game. Its advantage is not initial technological supremacy. Its advantage is willingness to study, import, adapt, and institutionalize.

Strong areas over time should include:

- torpedo tactics;
- cruiser operations;
- naval training;
- gunnery discipline;
- fleet coordination;
- operational planning;
- adoption of foreign ship designs;
- gradual domestic shipbuilding;
- later wireless, fire control, naval aviation, and carrier doctrine if the timeline extends.

In 1880, however, Japan should still be technologically dependent in many high-end areas.

## **Doctrine-Technology Fit**

Japan should not adopt technology randomly. It should adopt what solves urgent regional problems:

- torpedoes to offset larger fleets;
- cruisers for scouting and Korea/China operations;
- modern guns for decisive regional engagements;
- coaling and logistics for short expeditionary campaigns;
- shipbuilding capacity for independence.

Japan’s technological development should therefore feel strategic and disciplined, not sprawling.

---

# **12\. Diplomatic Identity**

## **Revisionist But Cautious**

Japan wants revision of its international status. It wants recognition, treaty equality, and freedom from foreign coercion. But it knows it is not yet strong enough to challenge all great powers directly.

This creates a diplomatic personality combining caution and ambition.

Japan should:

- avoid premature confrontation with Britain;
- watch Russia carefully;
- compete with Qing China over Korea;
- learn from France and Britain;
- seek useful foreign advisors and ship contracts;
- use modernization to gain diplomatic respect;
- exploit moments when Western powers are distracted.

## **Britain as Model and Constraint**

Britain is both a model and a limit.

Japan admires British naval professionalism and may seek British-built ships or training influence. But Britain is also the global maritime hegemon whose approval or opposition matters enormously.

Japan should not begin as Britain’s enemy. Nor should it be a British client by default. The relationship should be pragmatic: Britain may value Japan as a stabilizing or useful regional counterweight, but will also resist threats to its own China trade and imperial routes.

## **Qing China as Immediate Rival**

Qing China should be Japan’s primary regional naval rival in 1880\. The competition is not only about fleets; it is about Korea, prestige, modernization, and who defines East Asia’s future.

The Imo Incident and subsequent Chinese intervention in Korea reinforced Japanese concern that China could dominate Korea and limit Japan’s regional position.

## **Russia as Strategic Shadow**

Russia should be the looming long-term threat. Its Far Eastern expansion, access to northern waters, and continental mass create anxiety even before open conflict becomes likely.

Japan’s navy should increasingly consider Russia as the enemy that requires not just modernization, but strategic depth.

---

# **13\. Colonial and Overseas Policy Identity**

## **Security Perimeter Expansion**

Japan’s overseas policy should be modeled as expansion of a security perimeter.

Potential future objectives include:

- influence over Korea;
- southern island chains;
- Taiwan/Formosa;
- coaling access;
- treaty privileges;
- later Pacific interests;
- denial of hostile bases near Japan.

Japan should not expand randomly. Its expansion should follow a coherent geography of maritime security.

## **Small Empire, High Stakes**

Because Japan begins with few overseas possessions, each forward position matters disproportionately. A port, island, or coaling station can reshape Japan’s operational reach.

This should produce gameplay where Japan’s territorial gains are fewer than Britain’s but strategically transformative.

---

# **14\. Intelligence and Threat Perception**

Japan should be highly intelligence-sensitive because it cannot afford strategic surprise.

It should monitor:

- Qing fleet modernization;
- Chinese influence in Korea;
- Russian naval movements;
- British and French policy in East Asia;
- Western shipbuilding availability;
- Korean instability;
- treaty negotiations;
- foreign bases and coaling stations;
- technology transfers;
- domestic unrest affecting military budgets.

Japan’s intelligence priorities should be regional and practical. It does not need Britain’s global intelligence web; it needs excellent awareness of nearby rivals and foreign intervention risk.

---

# **15\. Social and Cultural Identity**

## **National Survival Through Modernization**

Japan’s modernization is existential. The memory of forced opening and Western pressure makes naval development emotionally and politically powerful. The navy symbolizes entry into the modern world, equality with the West, and protection from humiliation.

Japanese navalism should therefore be tied to:

- sovereignty;
- modernization;
- imperial loyalty;
- national discipline;
- technological learning;
- regional destiny;
- treaty revision;
- fear of subordination.

The arrival of Western naval power in the mid-19th century exposed Japan’s vulnerability and helped catalyze the Meiji transformation and rapid modernization. ,

## **Elite-Led Modernization**

Japan’s modernization is directed from above. The state should be able to pursue long-term modernization with discipline, but at social and fiscal cost.

This should produce a distinctive political feel:

- less public parliamentary volatility than France;
- less congressional reluctance than the United States;
- less imperial overconfidence than Britain;
- less army-dominated continental arrogance than Germany;
- more elite-driven urgency.

---

# **16\. AI Personality Definition**

If controlled by AI, Japan should behave as an **urgent regional modernizer**.

## **Strategic Priorities**

Japanese AI should prioritize:

1. defending the Home Islands;
2. building a modern regional fleet;
3. gaining influence over Korea;
4. countering Qing China;
5. preparing for Russian pressure;
6. acquiring modern ships and technology;
7. improving naval training;
8. expanding domestic shipbuilding capacity;
9. securing forward bases;
10. avoiding premature conflict with superior Western powers.

## **AI Behavioral Traits**

Japanese AI should be:

- disciplined;
- opportunistic;
- modernization-focused;
- regionally aggressive when ready;
- cautious toward Britain and other Western powers;
- highly sensitive to Korea;
- willing to concentrate limited forces;
- willing to invest heavily in training and readiness;
- strategically patient but capable of sudden war if opportunity appears.

## **AI Strategic Errors**

Japan should sometimes:

- overestimate what training can offset;
- underfund logistics while buying combat ships;
- provoke China before readiness is complete;
- underestimate Russia’s long-term threat;
- rely too heavily on imported warships;
- neglect domestic industrial bottlenecks;
- pursue prestige ships at the expense of infrastructure;
- assume short wars will solve long-term resource problems.

These errors should arise from Japan’s identity, not arbitrary weakness.

---

# **17\. Player Fantasy**

Playing Japan should feel like guiding a vulnerable island state through a forced-march transformation into a modern naval power.

The Japanese player fantasy is:

- master Western naval technology without becoming dependent;
- build a fleet under severe resource limits;
- defeat larger but less coherent regional rivals;
- secure Korea and nearby seas;
- earn recognition from Western powers;
- create domestic shipbuilding capacity;
- choose when to risk war;
- transform Japan from vulnerable modernizer into respected maritime empire.

The Japanese campaign should ask:

> Can you modernize fast enough to secure Japan’s independence and regional position before China, Russia, or the Western empires close the window of opportunity?

# **18\. Direct Comparison: Japan vs Britain, Germany, France, and the United States**

## **Strategic Identity**

**Britain:** Mature maritime hegemon defending a global system.  
**Germany:** Continental-industrial challenger seeking world-power recognition.  
**France:** Wounded great power restoring status through colonies, alliances, and innovation.  
**United States:** Secure continental republic awakening to sea power.  
**Japan:** Vulnerable island modernizer racing to secure regional sovereignty.

## **Naval Purpose**

**Britain:** Navy is the lifeline of empire.  
**Germany:** Navy is prestige, leverage, and eventual deterrence.  
**France:** Navy is compensation, colonial reach, and technological offset.  
**United States:** Navy is emerging tool of hemispheric and commercial expansion.  
**Japan:** Navy is survival, modernization, and regional ascent.

## **Main Anxiety**

**Britain:** Loss of global sea control.  
**Germany:** Encirclement and lack of status.  
**France:** Germany on land and Britain at sea.  
**United States:** European interference near American waters.  
**Japan:** Foreign domination of East Asia before Japan is strong enough to resist.

## **Empire**

**Britain:** Mature global operating system.  
**Germany:** Prestige colonies with weak logistics.  
**France:** Expanding colonial counterweight.  
**United States:** Informal influence and base-seeking before empire.  
**Japan:** Security perimeter expansion from the Home Islands outward.

## **Doctrine**

**Britain:** Command of the sea, blockade, trade protection.  
**Germany:** Concentrated fleet and home-water deterrence.  
**France:** Jeune École, commerce raiding, torpedoes, colonial cruisers.  
**United States:** Transition from coastal defense to Mahanian blue-water doctrine.  
**Japan:** Concentrated regional superiority, modernization, torpedo/cruiser leverage, and decisive local campaigns.

## **Technology**

**Britain:** Broad, mature, incremental.  
**Germany:** Methodical, industrial, technically focused.  
**France:** Experimental and disruptive.  
**United States:** Scalable and industrial-modernizing.  
**Japan:** Fast-learning, selective, import-dependent but determined to become autonomous.

## **Gameplay Feel**

**Britain:** Manage overstretched supremacy.  
**Germany:** Build concentrated relevance.  
**France:** Forge coherence from competing visions.  
**United States:** Convert latent power into maritime ambition.  
**Japan:** Modernize under pressure before the regional balance hardens against you.

# **19\. Design Constraints for Future Phases**

## **Geography Phase**

Japan must be modeled as a maritime island state whose strategic world centers on the Home Islands, Korea Strait, Yellow Sea, Sea of Japan, northern approaches, and routes toward China and the Pacific.

## **Territories Phase**

Japan should begin with limited overseas control. Future expansion should be security-driven and regionally coherent, especially around Korea, Taiwan/Formosa, and nearby island chains.

## **Resources Phase**

Japan must be resource-constrained relative to Britain, Germany, France, and the United States. Imported materials and industrial bottlenecks should matter.

## **Government Phase**

Japan must reflect Meiji constitutional monarchy, imperial authority, oligarchic leadership, emerging Diet politics, army-navy rivalry, and elite-directed modernization.

## **Industry Phase**

Japan must have rapid industrial growth potential but weak initial heavy industry compared with Western great powers.

## **Shipbuilding Phase**

Japan should rely partly on foreign construction and technology transfer early, with a long-term goal of domestic naval-industrial autonomy.

## **Naval Infrastructure Phase**

Japan should possess important home naval bases but limited forward infrastructure. Each new base should have major strategic value.

## **Technology Phase**

Japan should learn quickly, adopt foreign technology efficiently, and gain strong benefits from targeted modernization, but should not begin technologically independent.

## **Doctrine Phase**

Japan must emphasize concentrated regional operations, fleet readiness, torpedo craft, cruisers, and later decisive battle thinking as its fleet matures.

## **Diplomacy Phase**

Japan must be cautious toward Western powers, competitive with Qing China, increasingly wary of Russia, and strongly motivated by treaty revision and recognition.

## **Scenario Phase**

Japan’s January 1880 setup must reflect Meiji modernization, the new constitutional order, recent naval expansion pressures from Korean crises, and Japan’s unresolved regional rivalry with Qing China.

# **20\. Historical Assumptions**

## **Assumption 1 — Japan Is Not Yet a Great Naval Power**

Japan is treated as a rapidly modernizing but still resource-limited navy in 1880\. This follows the history of the IJN’s early reliance on training, imported ships, and gradual expansion after the Meiji Restoration. ,

## **Assumption 2 — Korea Is Central to Japanese Strategy**

Korea is assumed to be Japan’s key regional security concern because crises there demonstrated both Japan’s vulnerability and Qing China’s ability to intervene.

## **Assumption 3 — Japan’s Modernization Is Elite-Directed**

Japan’s government is assumed to be a Meiji constitutional empire with strong imperial and oligarchic leadership, not a fully parliamentary naval democracy. The Meiji Constitution and Imperial Diet define this transitional political structure. [\[ndl.go.jp\]](https://www.ndl.go.jp/modern/e/cha2/index.html),

## **Assumption 4 — Japan Learns Faster Than It Builds**

Japan’s strongest early advantage is institutional learning, not industrial mass. It can import, study, adapt, and train quickly, but domestic heavy industry and shipbuilding remain limiting factors in 1880\.

## **Assumption 5 — Japanese Expansion Begins as Security Strategy**

Japan’s expansionist logic is treated as primarily regional and security-driven at the start, rather than global or purely prestige-based.

> **The Empire of Japan in January 1880 is an urgent Meiji island modernizer: politically centralized, strategically insecure, resource-constrained, and determined to build a modern navy fast enough to secure Korea, resist Qing and Russian pressure, and force recognition as a sovereign great power in an imperial world.**
