# **Phase 1 — Canonical Nation Identity**

# **United States of America, January 1880**

# **1\. Canonical Summary**

The **United States of America** in January 1880 is a rapidly industrializing republic with enormous economic potential, vast continental resources, two ocean frontiers, and growing commercial interests beyond its shores. It is not yet a fully mature global naval power, and it does not possess a colonial empire comparable to Britain or France. Its navy is modernizing, but its strategic culture remains divided between older traditions of coastal defense, commerce protection, hemispheric security, and a newer blue-water vision.

The United States had neglected its navy after the Civil War, and by 1880 its navy ranked poorly by great-power standards; beginning in 1881, Congress supported modernization through steel-hulled, steam-powered, rifled-gun warships, laying the foundation for the “New Navy.”

The American identity in 1880 should therefore be:

> **The sleeping sea-power republic: geographically secure, industrially immense, politically cautious, doctrinally awakening, and capable of becoming a major naval power faster than older empires expect.**

The United States should begin weaker at sea than its future potential suggests. Its core gameplay arc is transformation.

---

# **2\. Strategic Identity**

## **The Secure Continental Republic**

Unlike France and Germany, the United States does not face an existential land threat from a nearby great-power army. Unlike Britain, it does not yet depend on a worldwide imperial network for national survival. Its continental position gives it strategic depth, internal resources, and relative freedom from Old World military pressures.

This produces a very specific strategic culture:

- no permanent fear of land invasion by a peer army;
- suspicion of large standing military establishments;
- emphasis on continental expansion and internal development;
- preference for hemispheric security over European entanglement;
- delayed recognition that trade and industry require naval reach;
- ability to mobilize vast resources once politically committed.

The United States is therefore secure enough to neglect naval power — and rich enough to build it rapidly once convinced.

## **The Two-Ocean Problem**

The United States has an Atlantic coast and a Pacific coast, but no canal connecting them in 1880\. This creates a defining strategic problem:

> **The United States has two ocean frontiers but cannot easily concentrate naval power between them.**

This is very different from Germany’s North Sea/Baltic problem. Germany’s seas are constrained and close to its industrial core. America’s seas are vast, separated by continental distance, and linked only by long routes around South America or future canal strategy.

American naval planning must therefore consider:

- Atlantic coast defense;
- Caribbean security;
- Gulf of Mexico interests;
- Pacific coast defense;
- Hawaii and Pacific access;
- possible conflict with Spain;
- British power in Canada and the Caribbean;
- future need for an isthmian canal;
- protection of expanding overseas commerce.

The United States should feel geographically safe but operationally divided.

## **Strategic Dilemma**

The American strategic dilemma is:

> **The United States has the resources to become a naval giant, but must first decide that overseas naval power is compatible with republican identity.**

This is not Britain’s problem of overstretch, Germany’s problem of army-navy competition, or France’s problem of doctrinal fragmentation. America’s problem is political will.

The American player should often have the capacity to build more — but must justify why.

# **3\. Geographic Identity**

## **Continental Scale**

The United States is a continental power before it is a maritime empire. Its internal geography — railroads, rivers, industrial cities, agricultural regions, mining zones, ports, and immigration centers — gives it extraordinary long-term strength.

Its naval geography is defined by distance:

- the Atlantic connects the United States to Europe, the Caribbean, and South America;
- the Pacific connects it to Hawaii, East Asia, and future trans-Pacific ambitions;
- the Gulf of Mexico links southern ports to Caribbean strategy;
- Alaska and the North Pacific create later strategic possibilities;
- lack of an isthmian canal makes fleet redistribution slow and strategically costly.

This makes American naval strategy naturally interested in mobility, coaling, forward bases, and canal politics.

## **The Caribbean as Strategic Near Abroad**

For the United States, the Caribbean is not a distant colonial theater. It is the maritime approaches to the Gulf Coast, the Mississippi trade system, the southeastern seaboard, and any future canal route.

Spain’s remaining Caribbean colonies — especially Cuba and Puerto Rico — matter because they sit close to American strategic space. By 1880, Spain still retained Cuba and Puerto Rico, while later Cuban unrest and Spanish repression would become central to the Spanish-American War crisis. , [\[pressbooks...hawaii.edu\]](https://pressbooks-dev.oer.hawaii.edu/ushistory/chapter/the-spanish-american-war-and-overseas-empire/)

The game should therefore treat the Caribbean as America’s first likely overseas naval theater.

## **Pacific Possibility**

In 1880, the Pacific is not yet as central to American identity as it will become, but the strategic logic is already present. The Pacific coast needs defense, American trade with Asia is attractive, Hawaii matters as a potential stepping stone, and East Asian markets may draw American attention.

Mahan himself promoted American control over Hawaii, and his wider sea-power thinking connected naval power, commerce, bases, and national greatness.

The United States should therefore have a latent Pacific path even before acquiring formal overseas empire.

---

# **4\. Imperial Identity**

## **Not Yet a Formal Overseas Empire**

In January 1880, the United States is not yet a colonial naval empire in the British or French sense. It has continental expansion behind it, but not yet a large overseas colonial system.

This is critical.

America should not begin with Britain’s global base network, France’s colonial empire, or Germany’s imperial prestige colonies. Instead, it begins with:

- continental resources;
- commercial growth;
- missionary and business networks;
- Pacific interests;
- Caribbean concern;
- ideological tension over imperialism;
- capacity for sudden overseas expansion.

The Spanish-American War of 1898 later became a major turning point in American overseas empire, resulting in U.S. acquisition of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines and a protectorate relationship over Cuba.

In 1880, this future is plausible but not inevitable.

## **Informal Empire Before Formal Empire**

American influence should initially be expressed less through colonies and more through:

- trade;
- investment;
- treaty rights;
- naval visits;
- protection of citizens abroad;
- Monroe Doctrine logic;
- Caribbean influence;
- Pacific coaling access;
- missionary and commercial pressure.

This gives the United States a different imperial path. It may become an empire, but it begins as a republic with expanding interests and ambiguous intentions.

## **Anti-Imperial Tension**

Any American overseas expansion should create domestic controversy. Unlike Britain, where empire is a mature national structure, or France, where empire can be framed as restoration and prestige, the United States must reconcile expansion with republican anti-colonial self-image.

This tension should become a major design pillar in later government and diplomacy phases.

# **5\. Political Identity**

## **Federal Republic with Congressional Naval Control**

The United States is a federal constitutional republic. Naval expansion requires congressional authorization, public support, executive leadership, and bureaucratic advocacy. The president cannot simply decree a giant battle fleet into existence.

American naval policy should therefore be shaped by:

- Congress;
- regional interests;
- party politics;
- fiscal caution;
- business pressure;
- naval reformers;
- public opinion;
- newspapers;
- crises abroad;
- ideological debates over empire;
- constitutional and republican suspicion of militarism.

Compared with Britain, American naval politics are less about maintaining accepted supremacy and more about persuading a continental electorate that sea power matters.

## **Political Reluctance and Crisis Mobilization**

The United States should be slow to commit in normal times but capable of rapid political mobilization after a crisis.

This is a central gameplay distinction:

- Britain constantly funds naval strength because its empire requires it.
- Germany funds naval strength if elites choose world-power ambition.
- France funds naval strength through a mix of rivalry, colonies, and doctrine.
- The United States funds naval strength when commerce, prestige, security, or public crisis makes naval power politically unavoidable.

The sinking of USS _Maine_ in 1898 later helped trigger war with Spain, showing how a naval crisis could rapidly transform public and political conditions. ,

## **Expansionist Debate**

America’s political identity should include competing schools:

- traditional continentalists;
- coastal defense advocates;
- business expansionists;
- naval professionals;
- Mahanian sea-power advocates;
- anti-imperialists;
- missionaries and humanitarian interventionists;
- Monroe Doctrine hardliners;
- Pacific commercialists;
- isolationist fiscal conservatives.

American strategy should be powerful once unified, but unity should not be assumed.

# **6\. Economic and Industrial Identity**

## **Industrial Giant in Formation**

The United States is rapidly becoming one of the world’s great industrial economies. Its long-term naval potential is enormous because it has:

- coal;
- iron and steel;
- railroads;
- machine production;
- population growth;
- immigration;
- finance;
- agricultural surplus;
- expanding cities;
- large domestic markets;
- private industrial corporations.

By the 1880s, the United States was emerging as a major industrial power, and the modernization of the navy was connected to steel, steam machinery, and rifled guns.

The United States should not begin with Britain’s naval-industrial maturity, but its growth potential should be extraordinary.

## **Industry Before Doctrine**

America’s industrial base is ahead of its naval doctrine and global commitments. This is one of its defining features.

Britain has doctrine, bases, and ships already integrated.  
Germany has industrial ambition tied to state planning.  
France has advanced ideas but uneven integration.  
The United States has the industrial capacity to build a great navy before it fully agrees on what that navy is for.

This creates a unique gameplay arc: converting industrial mass into strategic purpose.

## **Merchant and Commercial Expansion**

American commerce is expanding, but the merchant marine and overseas station network do not yet match Britain’s maritime system. The United States needs markets, but it has not yet built the naval infrastructure of a commercial empire.

Mahan’s 1880 work resonated with American leaders partly because it connected sea power with overseas markets, commerce, bases, and national greatness. ,

The United States should therefore be increasingly sensitive to trade access and market protection, especially in the Caribbean, Latin America, and the Pacific.

# **7\. Naval Institutional Identity**

## **The New Navy**

The U.S. Navy in 1880 is in transition from the post-Civil War era of neglect into the modern “New Navy.” The old wooden, sail, and Civil War legacy fleet is being replaced by steel, steam, rifled guns, professional planning, and modern naval education. Beginning in 1881, Congress supported modernization programs, and by the 1880s the U.S. Navy had moved toward all-steel and steam warships.

The U.S. Navy should feel:

- modernizing;
- intellectually ambitious;
- materially incomplete;
- increasingly professional;
- still politically vulnerable;
- eager to prove relevance;
- divided between coastal defense and blue-water missions.

## **Naval War College and Professionalization**

The Naval War College and figures such as Alfred Thayer Mahan are central to America’s naval identity. Mahan’s _The Influence of Sea Power upon History_ was published in 1880 and became one of the most influential works of naval strategy, arguing for the relationship between sea power, commerce, bases, strong fleets, and national greatness. ,

This means America’s naval institution should be intellectually ahead of its fleet. It is already thinking about sea power before it possesses full sea power.

That should feel different from Britain:

- Britain has lived sea power.
- America is theorizing sea power.

## **Professional Insecurity**

The U.S. Navy should have institutional insecurity similar to Germany’s navy, but for different reasons.

Germany’s navy is insecure because the army dominates and Britain is superior.  
America’s navy is insecure because the country is wealthy but unconvinced it needs a peacetime battle fleet.

This creates a naval officer corps that is reformist, persuasive, and increasingly strategic.

---

# **8\. Naval Strategic Doctrine**

## **From Coastal Defense to Sea Control**

In January 1880, American doctrine should be in transition.

Older American naval assumptions emphasize:

- coastal defense;
- harbor protection;
- commerce protection;
- limited overseas squadrons;
- avoidance of European entanglements.

Newer Mahanian thinking emphasizes:

- concentrated battle fleets;
- command of the sea;
- decisive fleet action;
- overseas bases;
- protection and expansion of commerce;
- naval power as a condition of great-power status.

Mahan’s work encouraged large capital ships, decisive battle thinking, and blockade concepts, and it influenced naval strategy internationally.

## **Hemispheric Sea Power**

America’s naval doctrine should initially be more hemispheric than global.

Core concerns:

- defend the U.S. coastline;
- dominate the Caribbean;
- prevent hostile European bases near the American mainland;
- protect Gulf approaches;
- secure future canal routes;
- protect Pacific coast access;
- gain coaling stations;
- deter European intervention in the Western Hemisphere.

The United States should not begin by trying to police the entire world. Its first naval ambition should be regional maritime dominance.

## **War Planning Against Britain**

Although later history makes Anglo-American friendship seem natural, in the 1880s Britain could still appear as a plausible naval opponent in American planning. U.S. naval professionals developed war plans involving Great Britain in 1880 and later Naval War College exercises in 1894 and 1895\.

This matters for design: Britain and the United States should not begin as automatic allies. They share language, trade, and political affinities, but they also have disputes over Canada, fisheries, hemispheric influence, and naval power.

# **9\. Ship Design Identity**

## **General Design Philosophy**

American ship design should emphasize modernization, industrial practicality, heavy engineering, and experimentation within political limits.

Early American design tendencies should include:

- coastal defense influence;
- protected cruisers;
- armored cruisers;
- modern steel construction;
- interest in heavy guns;
- gradual battleship development;
- endurance needs for two-ocean operations;
- experimentation caused by limited recent experience;
- growing desire for blue-water capability.

The New Navy modernization emphasized steel hulls, steam engines, and large rifled guns as part of the shift away from the neglected post-Civil War fleet.

## **Battleships**

American battleships should initially be politically and strategically contested. A battle fleet is central to Mahanian doctrine, but the United States has not yet fully accepted permanent blue-water fleet policy.

American battleship design should therefore evolve from:

- coast-defense logic;
- limited-range early battleships;
- harbor and Atlantic defense;
- toward blue-water, ocean-capable battle fleets.

The United States should not begin with Britain’s battleship tradition or Germany’s later systematic battle-fleet program. It should begin with ambition catching up to industrial possibility.

## **Cruisers**

Cruisers are important early American ships because they fit several politically acceptable missions:

- showing the flag;
- protecting citizens abroad;
- commerce protection;
- Caribbean presence;
- Pacific diplomacy;
- scouting;
- limited overseas operations;
- training a modern officer corps.

Cruisers help bridge the gap between coastal republic and global navy.

## **Auxiliary and Logistics Ships**

America should have strong future need for colliers, repair ships, coaling stations, and overseas support. Because the United States lacks Britain’s global base network, logistics should become central once it tries to operate beyond home waters.

This should become especially important in later phases.

---

# **10\. Industrial and Shipbuilding Identity**

## **Latent Shipbuilding Power**

The United States has enormous industrial potential but is still developing a modern naval shipbuilding ecosystem in 1880\. Its commercial and industrial base can support rapid expansion, but naval construction requires specialized skills, armor production, gun manufacture, dockyard capacity, and political commitment.

The U.S. should therefore begin as:

- underdeveloped relative to potential;
- capable of rapid growth;
- dependent on domestic political authorization;
- increasingly able to produce modern steel warships;
- likely to improve rapidly through experience.

## **Private Industry and Federal Procurement**

American naval construction should be closely tied to private industry, federal contracts, congressional districts, and industrial lobbying. Warship programs should affect domestic politics because they bring jobs, technology, and prestige to shipbuilding regions.

Unlike Britain, where naval building is woven into imperial necessity, American naval building must justify itself as national modernization, coast defense, commerce protection, or great-power assertion.

## **Scaling Advantage**

Once the United States commits, its advantage should be scaling.

America may begin behind in naval tradition, but its industrial base should eventually support:

- large steel output;
- rapid shipyard expansion;
- gun and armor production;
- mass training;
- two-ocean naval construction;
- strong repair and modernization capacity.

This should make the United States dangerous in long campaigns.

# **11\. Technological Identity**

## **Modernizer, Not Traditionalist**

The United States should be technologically open because it is building a modern navy from a relatively weak base. It is not bound by centuries of capital-ship tradition like Britain, nor by France’s doctrinal factionalism, nor by Germany’s army-dominated imperial structure.

American technological identity should favor:

- modern steel hulls;
- steam machinery;
- heavy guns;
- naval education;
- gunnery reform;
- electrical systems;
- industrial standardization;
- fleet logistics;
- eventually fire control, aviation, submarines, and carriers later in the timeline.

## **Technology Through Industry**

America’s naval technology should be driven less by aristocratic tradition or colonial necessity and more by industrial capitalism, engineering, and federal procurement.

This gives the United States a long-term ability to leap forward once mature systems align.

Weaknesses in 1880:

- limited institutional experience;
- fewer overseas bases;
- immature naval bureaucracy compared with Britain;
- political interruptions;
- uncertainty over doctrine;
- dependence on congressional support.

Strengths:

- enormous industrial ceiling;
- willingness to adopt modern systems;
- expanding technical education;
- strong private-sector innovation;
- ability to scale once committed.

# **12\. Diplomatic Identity**

## **Hemispheric Power**

The United States should begin with a diplomatic identity centered on the Western Hemisphere. It is wary of European interference near its shores and increasingly willing to assert influence in the Caribbean and Latin America.

Its diplomacy should include:

- Monroe Doctrine logic;
- suspicion of European colonial presence in the Americas;
- interest in Cuba;
- concern over Caribbean routes;
- Pacific commercial ambition;
- cautious distance from European alliance systems;
- willingness to pressure weaker regional states.

## **Britain: Rival, Model, and Future Partner**

Britain should occupy an ambiguous place in American diplomacy.

Britain is:

- the world’s naval model;
- a major trading partner;
- a possible rival in Canada and the Caribbean;
- the strongest navy the United States must consider;
- culturally familiar but strategically dangerous.

American naval professionals prepared war plans involving Britain in the 1880s, showing that Britain should not begin as a scripted friend.

Over time, Anglo-American rapprochement should be possible, but it must emerge from diplomacy and shared interests, not inevitability.

## **Spain: Declining Nearby Empire**

Spain should matter to the United States because its remaining colonies sit within areas of growing American concern. By 1880, Spain still held Cuba and Puerto Rico, and later conflict over Cuba became central to the Spanish-American War. , [\[pressbooks...hawaii.edu\]](https://pressbooks-dev.oer.hawaii.edu/ushistory/chapter/the-spanish-american-war-and-overseas-empire/)

Spain is therefore America’s most plausible early naval opponent, but not because war is scripted. It is plausible because geography, Cuban unrest, press politics, humanitarian rhetoric, and expansionist pressures can converge.

# **13\. Colonial and Overseas Policy Identity**

## **Expansion Without Consensus**

The United States should have overseas ambitions before it has consensus about empire.

Possible American overseas objectives:

- coaling stations;
- Caribbean influence;
- Hawaiian access or annexation path;
- Pacific trade;
- canal route security;
- protection of citizens and missionaries;
- Spanish colonial crises;
- informal economic influence.

But every step toward formal empire should create domestic tension.

## **Bases Before Colonies**

America’s first naval-imperial logic should be about bases rather than large colonies. The Mahanian logic of sea power emphasizes that fleets, commerce, and overseas stations are connected. ,

The United States therefore needs:

- Caribbean stations;
- Pacific coaling points;
- repair access;
- canal strategy;
- forward presence.

This makes its overseas expansion more infrastructure-driven than prestige-driven at first.

# **14\. Intelligence and Threat Perception**

American threat perception should initially focus on:

- European naval presence in the Caribbean;
- British naval superiority;
- Spanish colonial instability;
- protection of U.S. citizens abroad;
- Pacific access;
- future canal routes;
- coastal vulnerability;
- foreign fleets near American waters.

Unlike France, America is not haunted by a lost province. Unlike Germany, it is not surrounded by hostile land powers. Unlike Britain, it is not trying to monitor every sea route.

America’s intelligence problem is narrower at first — but expands rapidly if the United States acquires overseas commitments.

# **15\. Social and Cultural Identity**

## **Republican Anti-Militarism vs National Ambition**

American society in 1880 contains a strong tension between republican suspicion of militarism and growing national confidence.

Naval expansion can be justified through:

- defense of commerce;
- protection of coasts;
- protection of citizens;
- humanitarian intervention;
- national prestige;
- markets overseas;
- technological modernization;
- great-power respect.

But opponents can frame naval expansion as:

- imperialism;
- militarism;
- waste;
- entanglement in foreign quarrels;
- betrayal of republican principles.

This domestic tension should be central to America’s identity.

## **The Closing Frontier and Outward Turn**

Mahan’s sea-power arguments resonated in the 1880s with American concerns over markets, expansion, and the limits of continental opportunity. The U.S. State Department’s historical summary links Mahan’s influence with debates over securing international markets and America’s outward turn in the 1880s.

This provides an important cultural arc:

> America turns outward not because it has always been a naval empire, but because industrial growth and continental completion make overseas markets and sea power more attractive.

# **16\. AI Personality Definition**

If controlled by AI, the United States should behave as a **cautious industrial giant awakening to sea power**.

## **Strategic Priorities**

American AI should prioritize:

1. coastal defense;
2. Caribbean security;
3. industrial growth;
4. naval modernization;
5. avoiding premature European entanglement;
6. protection of commerce;
7. Pacific access;
8. canal-route interests;
9. crisis-driven fleet expansion;
10. eventual blue-water capability.

## **AI Behavioral Traits**

American AI should be:

- slow-starting;
- crisis-reactive;
- industrially powerful;
- politically constrained;
- commercially motivated;
- suspicious of European colonial presence nearby;
- increasingly confident over time;
- capable of sudden naval acceleration;
- prone to two-ocean planning problems.

## **AI Strategic Errors**

American AI should sometimes:

- underfund the navy during peace;
- overreact after public crises;
- build ships without enough bases;
- split forces between oceans prematurely;
- underestimate logistics;
- assume industrial capacity can solve operational problems instantly;
- provoke Spain or another weaker empire before fully prepared;
- ignore Pacific commitments while focusing on the Caribbean, or vice versa.

These mistakes should arise from America’s identity, not arbitrary weakness.

# **17\. Player Fantasy**

Playing the United States should feel like guiding a continental republic into the age of sea power.

The American player fantasy is:

- transform industrial abundance into naval strength;
- decide whether to remain hemispheric or go global;
- build a modern navy from an underdeveloped starting point;
- dominate the Caribbean;
- secure future canal routes;
- enter the Pacific strategically;
- manage anti-imperial domestic politics;
- outbuild older powers if given time;
- choose whether American sea power serves defense, commerce, empire, or global order.

The American campaign should ask:

> Can you convince a secure continental republic that control of distant seas is worth the political, moral, and financial cost?

# **18\. Direct Comparison: United States vs Britain vs Germany vs France**

## **Strategic Identity**

**Britain:** Mature maritime hegemon defending a global imperial system.  
**Germany:** Continental-industrial challenger deciding whether to seek world-power status through a concentrated fleet.  
**France:** Wounded old great power seeking restoration through colonies, alliances, and naval innovation.  
**United States:** Secure industrial republic awakening to overseas sea power.

## **Naval Purpose**

**Britain:** Navy is national lifeline.  
**Germany:** Navy is prestige and strategic leverage.  
**France:** Navy is compensation, colonial reach, and technological equalizer.  
**United States:** Navy is emerging instrument of hemispheric defense, commercial expansion, and future great-power status.

## **Main Anxiety**

**Britain:** Loss of command over global sea routes.  
**Germany:** Encirclement and lack of recognition.  
**France:** German threat plus British maritime superiority.  
**United States:** Foreign influence near American waters and inability to protect expanding interests.

## **Empire**

**Britain:** Mature global operating system.  
**Germany:** Prestige colonies with weak logistics.  
**France:** Expanding colonial counterweight and strategic base system.  
**United States:** Informal influence and base-seeking before formal overseas empire.

## **Doctrine**

**Britain:** Command of the sea, blockade, trade protection.  
**Germany:** Concentrated fleet, home-water deterrence, future risk strategy.  
**France:** Jeune École, commerce raiding, torpedoes, colonial cruisers.  
**United States:** Transition from coastal defense to Mahanian blue-water sea control.

## **Technology**

**Britain:** Broad, proven, incremental, conservative because dominant.  
**Germany:** Methodical, industrial, focused, technically intense.  
**France:** Experimental, disruptive, doctrinally unstable.  
**United States:** Industrial-modernizing, scalable, initially inexperienced but fast-learning.

## **Gameplay Feel**

**Britain:** Manage overstretched supremacy.  
**Germany:** Build concentrated relevance.  
**France:** Forge coherence from strategic contradictions.  
**United States:** Convert latent power into maritime ambition.

# **19\. Design Constraints for Future Phases**

## **Geography Phase**

The United States must be modeled as a two-ocean continental power with major operational separation between Atlantic and Pacific commitments.

## **Territories Phase**

The United States should begin with few overseas possessions compared with Britain and France; future overseas expansion should be plausible but not assumed.

## **Resources Phase**

American domestic resources must be immense, especially industrial raw materials, but overseas naval logistics should initially be limited.

## **Government Phase**

American government must include congressional budget authority, federal politics, public opinion, anti-imperial debate, crisis mobilization, and regional interests.

## **Industry Phase**

The United States must have very high industrial growth potential, especially in steel, machinery, shipbuilding, rail logistics, and later mass production.

## **Shipbuilding Phase**

American shipbuilding should begin behind Britain in naval maturity but possess exceptional scaling potential once funded.

## **Naval Infrastructure Phase**

The United States should have strong home-port potential but weak global infrastructure; coaling stations, canal access, and overseas bases must become strategic priorities.

## **Technology Phase**

The United States should be strong in modernization and industrial scaling, with increasing advantages as doctrine, industry, and funding align.

## **Doctrine Phase**

American doctrine must begin in transition: coastal defense and commerce protection giving way to Mahanian sea control and blue-water fleet concepts.

## **Diplomacy Phase**

The United States must be hemispheric, wary of European intervention, not automatically allied with Britain, and likely to clash with declining colonial powers near its sphere.

## **Scenario Phase**

The January 1880 scenario must reflect the New Navy transition, Mahan’s intellectual emergence, limited overseas commitments, and growing but unresolved outward-looking ambition.

# **20\. Historical Assumptions**

## **Assumption 1 — America Is Not Yet a Finished Naval Great Power**

The United States is treated as modernizing but incomplete in 1880\. This is supported by the post-Civil War naval decline and the modernization program begun in the 1880s.

## **Assumption 2 — Mahan Is Available as an Intellectual Catalyst**

Mahan’s _The Influence of Sea Power upon History_ was published in 1880 and is treated as a major doctrinal catalyst, but not as an instant transformation of American policy.

## **Assumption 3 — The United States Is Hemispheric Before It Is Global**

The United States begins with strong Caribbean and Western Hemisphere concerns, while Pacific and global ambitions are emerging but not yet fully institutionalized.

## **Assumption 4 — Britain Is Not Automatically Friendly**

Because U.S. naval professionals planned for possible war with Britain in the 1880s, Anglo-American friendship should be possible but not scripted.

## **Assumption 5 — American Power Is Latent and Scalable**

The United States’ defining strength is not starting naval mass but the ability to turn industrial, financial, and demographic power into naval strength once political will exists.
